How to read the airborne estimate: roughly 45% of emitted CO₂ stays in the atmosphere on multi-year timescales; the ocean and land take up the rest. That fraction varies year to year, so the airborne stock is an estimate, and the observed concentration is the check against it. 1 ppm of CO₂ ≈ 7.81 Gt CO₂ (2.13 Gt of carbon × 44/12).

What each column means. Net land-use change (Mt) — net CO₂ from land-use change in the latest year: a positive number means the country's land is a net source, a negative number means it is a net sink. Source or sink — whether the country's land overall adds CO₂ to the air or removes it.

Method: net land-use-change CO₂ as published in the dataset (land_use_change_co2). Identity check — fossil CO₂ + net land-use change = CO₂ including land-use change — holds for the world and for every country with data (largest difference 0.005 Mt, rounding). 194 of 220 countries have a value for 2025; the 26 without are mostly small territories, plus Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macao. A missing value is a gap, not zero.

Methods, model assumptions & sources

What is counted

Territorial CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry: coal, oil, gas, flaring, cement and other industrial processes, produced within a country's borders. This is the headline figure in every chart and table on this page.

Sector split (country detail)

Each country's latest-year total is split into six sectors from the same dataset — coal, oil, gas, cement, flaring, other industry — as published in the Our World in Data columns coal_co2, oil_co2, gas_co2, cement_co2, flaring_co2, other_industry_co2. A blank sector column in the source is 0 in Our World in Data's accounting: the six sectors sum to the fossil total for every country (largest difference 0.002 Mt, rounding). No end-use sectors (transport, buildings, etc.) are shown — the dataset does not carry them per country, and they are not invented here.

The tap point — how it is judged

For each sector the panel rates three things, on a 1–3 scale, and multiplies them by the sector's share of the country's total: reduction potential (how much of that sector's CO₂ the available alternatives can remove), ease of transition (how fast and simply the switch can be made), and cost (3 = cheaper than the incumbent, 2 = adds slight cost, 1 = extensive innovation still needed). The highlighted tap point is the sector with the highest score — the lowest friction and highest yield — not necessarily the largest share. The ratings are a transparent judgement, not a hidden score; the table shows every rating, so the reader can disagree with the weights. Basis, per sector:

  • Coal — potential high, ease high, cheaper than the incumbent, commercial. In 2024, 91% of new utility-scale renewable projects delivered power cheaper than the cheapest new fossil alternative; solar PV was 41% cheaper and onshore wind 53% cheaper (IRENA, Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024, 2025). Cost of the solution: onshore wind $34/MWh, solar PV $43/MWh — global weighted-average LCOE, 2024 (IRENA 2025, measured project data).
  • Oil — potential high, ease medium, adds slight cost, commercial. Electric cars were ~18% of 2023 car sales (IEA, Global EV Outlook 2024); battery costs are down ~93% since 2010 and lifetime cost parity with petrol cars is reached across much of Europe — but the upfront price is still higher and fleet turnover and charging take time. Cost of the solution: no single $/t CO₂ figure is published; the natural unit is about $1,200 extra build cost against about $4,700 fuel savings over 8 years (ICCT 2023, estimate).
  • Gas — potential medium, ease medium, adds slight cost, commercial. Heat pumps are mature and commercial (IEA, The Future of Heat Pumps, 2022: heat pumps avoided about 7 billion m³ of gas use in the EU in 2025); renewable power can displace gas-fired power, but high-temperature industrial heat still needs hydrogen or electrification that is not yet routine. Cost of the solution: no single $/t CO₂ figure is published; heat pumps cost 2–4× a gas boiler upfront but typically $300/yr (US) to $900/yr (Europe) less to run (IEA 2022, estimate).
  • Cement — potential medium, ease low, extensive innovation still needed, early-stage. About 60% of cement CO₂ is process emissions from limestone calcination, untouched by switching the fuel; CCUS on kilns costs roughly $100–200/t CO₂ and is at demonstration scale (TRL 6–9), with near-zero-emission cement under 1% of the market (IEA Cement Technology Roadmap; WEF Net-Zero Industry Tracker 2023). Lower-clinker cements (e.g. LC3) can cut ~35–40% with existing kilns. First full-scale plant: Brevik, Norway, 400,000 t CO₂/yr, operating since 2025.
  • Flaring — potential low, ease high, cheaper than the incumbent, commercial. The CO₂ share is small, but ending routine flaring is among the cheapest abatement anywhere: the IEA estimates ~40% of oil-and-gas methane abatement is available at no net cost, and 74% at $10/t CO₂e or less, because the captured gas has market value (IEA Global Methane Tracker 2023; IEA Sustainable Recovery 2020), and ~90% of flaring is technically avoidable (World Bank Zero Routine Flaring by 2030).
  • Other industry — potential medium, ease low, extensive innovation still needed, early-stage. Hydrogen-based steel (H₂-DRI) is at demonstration / early commercial scale and still uneconomical at scale, with many proposed projects cancelled or paused; the chemistry (hydrogen as reductant, not just fuel) has no cheap substitute yet (IEA Iron & Steel Technology Roadmap; Project Drawdown). Cost of the solution: green steel premium ~$200–300/t steel at today's green-hydrogen prices; break-even needs hydrogen at $1.63/kg or less (Rosner et al. 2023, estimate).

Natural net carbon

Net land-use-change CO₂ as published (land_use_change_co2): deforestation and other land-use emissions minus regrowth and land sinks, for the latest year. Positive = the country's land is a net source; negative = a net sink. No gross split is published in this dataset, so the section shows net only. The identity fossil + net land-use change = CO₂ including land-use change holds for the world (38,995 + 4,352 = 43,347 Mt in 2025) and for every country with data.

What is not in the headline figure

Land-use change (mainly deforestation) is excluded from the headline totals and shown separately (world: 4,352 Mt in 2025; cumulative 897,551 Mt). Other greenhouse gases (methane, nitrous oxide) and consumption-based emissions (emissions embedded in imports) are not counted here.

Model & estimate assumptions

  • Live counter: the 2025 world rate (38,995 Mt / year) is spread evenly across the year — 1,237 tonnes per second — and applied to 2026 until a newer annual total is published. It is an accounting spread, not a real-time measurement; real emissions vary by season, day and hour.
  • Cumulative live total: cumulative fossil CO₂ at the end of 2025 (1,886,833 Mt) plus the 2026 estimate from 1 January to now, at the same even rate.
  • Airborne estimate: 45% of cumulative emissions are assumed to remain in the atmosphere (the long-run airborne fraction in the Global Carbon Budget); the rest is taken up by ocean and land. 1 ppm CO₂ ≈ 7.81 Gt CO₂. Both are approximations — the observed Mauna Loa concentration is the measured check.
  • YoY %: (this year − last year) / last year × 100, from the annual series. Early years (before ~1900 for many countries) are sparser and less certain; a missing year is a gap, not zero.
  • Rounding: figures are shown to the nearest Mt / 0.1% where shown; underlying values are in Mt in data.json.
